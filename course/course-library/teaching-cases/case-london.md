# London: A Multi-Century Struggle for Sustainable Development in an Urban Environment

![The Great Fire of London, 1666, anonymous painting]({{ '/assets/images/cases/london.jpg' | relative_url }})

**William C. Clark**

This teaching case sketches key episodes in the two-thousand-year history of the interactions between society and environment that have shaped the City of London and its immediate hinterlands — from Roman settlement through plague, fire, fuel crises, and industrialization to the modern metropolis. The result is an empirical puzzle for use in teaching and theorizing about the long-term coevolution of social-environmental systems, and about how such systems might be guided toward sustainability. Frameworks for thinking about that coevolution have multiplied in sustainability science; empirical cases that actually span multi-decade to multi-century timeframes remain rare. London is one of them.

A companion piece, [Looking Outward: Refocusing Attention on London's Hinterland](case-london-addendum.html), extends the case beyond the city's immediate surroundings to the distant places and resources on which London's growth depended.

## Using this case in the course

The London case is first assigned in [Unit 1.4](/course/part-1-conceptual-challenge/unit-1-4-anthropogenic-resources.html) (anthropogenic resources), alongside the shorter version in Matson et al. (2016, pp. 143–165), and returns in [Unit 2.1](/course/part-2-anthropocene/unit-2-1-system-dynamics.html) (system dynamics), [Unit 2.4](/course/part-2-anthropocene/unit-2-4-horizontal-connections.html) (horizontal connections), [Unit 3.3](/course/part-3-capacities/unit-3-3-measure.html) (measuring progress), and [Unit 3.4](/course/part-3-capacities/unit-3-4-adapt.html) (adaptation). Used this way, the case lets students watch the ideas of successive units accumulate around a single place — and practice the essential work of fitting general ideas to context. Sustainable development is a long, intergenerational process; cases that span generations let students watch development pathways — and efforts to bend them — unfold at the time scales that matter.
